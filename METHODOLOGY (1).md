# Methodology

**Summary:** The shared methodological framework underlying CeloHT's research areas.
**Purpose:** A common baseline so different research areas don't reinvent (or silently diverge on) basic methodological choices.
**Scope:** Applies to all research areas; individual areas may add area-specific methodological notes in `research/<area>/methodology.md`.
**Audience:** Researchers, reviewers.

## Research Types Used at CeloHT

| Type | When it's used | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Program monitoring data analysis | Ongoing, low-cost, high-frequency | Agent transaction volume trends |
| Survey research | Periodic, structured feedback | Post-training knowledge assessments |
| Interview / qualitative research | Depth over breadth, exploratory questions | Why some agents leave within 90 days |
| Field observation | Understanding real-world program operation | How cash-in/cash-out actually unfolds in practice |
| Comparative / benchmark research | Situating CeloHT against alternatives | Transaction cost comparison across payment methods |
| Ecological / environmental research | Reforestation-specific | Survival rate analysis, species suitability |
| Policy / regulatory analysis | Understanding the operating environment | Digital payment regulation review |

## Choosing a Method

The method follows the question, not the other way around — a question about *why* agents leave calls for interviews, not a bigger survey. Every proposal (see [RESEARCH_GUIDELINES.md](./RESEARCH_GUIDELINES.md#proposing-new-research)) should justify method choice against the specific question being asked.

## Sampling

Where sampling is used (surveys, interviews), the methodology document states: the sampling frame, the sampling method (random, purposive, convenience — and why), the achieved sample size, and how representative the sample is likely to be of the broader population. Convenience sampling (common in early-stage field research) is disclosed as such, not dressed up as random.

## Baseline and Comparison

Where feasible, findings are reported against a baseline (before/after) or comparison group (participants vs. non-participants), rather than as a standalone snapshot — a standalone number rarely tells you whether a program caused anything.

## Confounding and Alternative Explanations

Every finding that implies a causal relationship ("training improved X") should also name plausible alternative explanations and, where possible, address them — self-selection into training being the most common one CeloHT's research needs to account for.

## Mixed Methods

Many CeloHT questions benefit from combining quantitative program data with qualitative interviews — e.g. transaction data shows *what* happened, interviews help explain *why*. Mixed-methods studies document both components' methodology separately.

## Statistical Standards

Where inferential statistics are used, report effect sizes and confidence intervals, not just p-values or point estimates alone. See [STATISTICS.md](./STATISTICS.md) *(companion document, research area-specific statistical notes)*.

## Field Research Considerations

CeloHT's field research happens in real communities with real constraints — see [FIELD_RESEARCH.md](./FIELD_RESEARCH.md) for logistics, safety, and community-relationship considerations specific to conducting research in this context.
